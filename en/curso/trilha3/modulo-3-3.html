1

🪦 Disarm the “loop died”

In July 2026, the idea circulated that “loop engineering is dead; now it’s graph engineering.” The phrase sounds like a new era, but technically it makes no sense: a loop already is a graph — a graph whose path returns to a previous node. You don't "graduate" from a loop to a graph as if you were switching tools.

What actually happens is composition: you compose loops into graphs when a loop is no longer enough—and that has a real cost; it is not just a free upgrade. You pay with more prompts to write, a state schema (the format of the data flowing through the system) is more elaborate, and failure modes that a standalone loop had never (a merge that swallows data, routing that doesn’t converge).

🆕 New here? Two words before you continue

  • State machine: an old way (from long before AI) of describing a system as a fixed set of “states” and the rules that say when it changes from one to another.
  • FOMO: "fear of missing out" — the fear of falling behind because everyone is talking about a new term. It's the fuel for this topic's hype cycle.

⚠️ The hype cycle in numbers

Loop engineering was declared "dead" after about six weeks as a popular term. Every week, a new vocabulary is announced as mandatory and the previous one is declared obsolete. If you change architectures every time a term changes, you never finish anything — you just chase labels.

What to look at: the loop doesn’t disappear inside the larger graph — it stays there, intact, as one of the nodes. What the graph does is place other nodes (A, B, C) around it and define how to enter and exit that loop. Nothing was replaced; one more node was added.

🗂️ Separate the knowledge graph from the execution graph

The most common debate mistake—even in posts that seem technical—is mixing up two different topics that have only the word “graph” in common. GraphRAG e knowledge graph are graphs of information: the nodes are entities or documents, the edges are relationships (“details,” “contradicts”), and an agent query this graph to answer a question.

Graph engineering, the subject of this course, is a graph of execution: the nodes are units of work (agents, model calls, code), the edges mean “what runs next,” and an agent goes through this graph to perform a task. They are data structures with the same technical name, applied to opposite problems.

🆕 New here? Two words before you continue

  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): a technique where the model searches a database for relevant passages before answering and uses them as context. "Retrieval-augmented generation."
  • GraphRAG: a RAG variant where the queried database is organized as a graph of entities and relationships, instead of just standalone text chunks.
  • Knowledge graph: a database where information becomes nodes (entities) and edges (relationships between them) — Google has used one for its search panels for over a decade.

What to look at: notice the verb under each side. Querying and traversing are different operations. A knowledge graph sits still, waiting for a question; an execution graph moves on its own, step by step, until the task is done. Confusing the two makes you think learning GraphRAG teaches you to orchestrate agents — it doesn't.

✓ It's a knowledge graph when

  • ✓The goal is to answer “who is connected to whom”
  • ✓The graph is queried, not executed step by step
  • ✓And a form of RAG — better search, not orchestration

✓ It's an execution graph when

  • ✓The goal is “what runs after what”
  • ✓The agent traverses the graph to complete a task
  • ✓This is the subject of this course — real graph engineering

Key concepts

Same name, different things

Knowledge vs. execution

GraphRAG and RAG

It's not a replacement

Query vs. traverse

🕵️ Check the assignments

Three specific claims circulated during the July 2026 debate saying that major companies “adopted graph engineering.” None of the three holds up when you check the primary source—that is, what the company itself actually announced.

1

"Graph engineering replaced RAG at Microsoft"

Microsoft published research on GraphRAG, a context retrieval technique.

GraphRAG is a form of RAG, not a discipline that replaces RAG. It’s a knowledge graph (topic 2), not an execution graph.

2

"Stanford adopted graph engineering" (via DSPy)

DSPy: Stanford research framework that automatically optimizes the prompts and parameters of a program that uses language models.

DSPy optimizes programs of a language model — it has nothing to do with knowledge graphs or a discipline called “graph engineering.”

3

"Anthropic launched graph engineering"

Anthropic uses code-written multi-agent orchestration in some of its own systems.

Using orchestration in code is very different from announcing a new discipline with that name. Anthropic did not announce “graph engineering” as a concept.

💡 Practical tip

Whenever a claim has the format "Company X adopted Y", ask: did this appear in an ad, post, or paper of its own company X, or just in a third-party post repeating what another post said? If it's the second option, treat it as a rumor until you find the primary source.

Key concepts

Microsoft

GraphRAG is a form of RAG

Stanford / DSPy

Optimize the program, not the graph

Anthropic

Orchestration in code ≠ discipline

Primary source

4

📉 Be wary of a number without a source

Two numbers circulated with the hype: “18% more accuracy” and “85% lower cost” for graph-based systems. They were cited as if they were a general property of any orchestrated graph system. They aren't. They came from a baseline specific: a study in a narrow domain, reading industrial diagrams.

A result from a narrow domain doesn’t become a general rule just because it was retweeted without context. The practical rule: before repeating a performance number, ask which experiment it came from, compared against what baseline (the baseline comparison) and the specific task.

🆕 New here? One word before you continue

  • Baseline: the “point of comparison” for an experiment — what you measure the new result against. “18% better” only means something if you know 18% better than what.

📊 Where the numbers that circulated came from

  • Domain: information extraction from industrial diagrams (not "agents in general")
  • Comparison: a specific traditional RAG pipeline as a baseline
  • Scope: one study, not a systematic review of multiple studies
  • Correct conclusion: "in is task, this graph approach beat this baseline" — not "graphs always win"

⚠️ Warning: an unqualified number is a red flag

If a post cites an improvement percentage without naming the study, the domain tested, and the baseline used, treat the number as decoration, not evidence. That also applies to the most impressive number you’ve ever seen.

Key concepts

18% / 85%

From a narrow domain

Baseline

Better than what?

Scope

One study ≠ a general rule

Default question

Experiment, baseline, task

5

🏛️ Recognize the prior art

None of the mechanics of “graph engineering” are new. State machine is a concept from decades ago. XState — a JavaScript library — formalized explicit, inspectable control flow years before the term appeared; the creator of XState himself took part in the debate, defending this idea all along, just under a different label.

LangGraph exposes agents as a StateGraph: you add nodes, connect edges, and state flows through them. Microsoft AutoGen and Google ADK provide graph-based workflows, agent routing, and multi-agent delegation—all of which predate the term "graph engineering." In the data world, DAGs have described workflows for decades. What’s new in 2026 is the vocabulary, not the structure.

🆕 New here? Two words before you continue

  • Prior art (“previous art”): a term borrowed from patents—everything that existed before a “new” idea was announced. If there’s prior art, the novelty is just a new label for something familiar.
  • State machine: already defined in topic 1 — a system with fixed states and transition rules between them.

What to look at: the highlight is on the latest milestone, not because it’s the most important, but because it’s the most recent and the least time-tested. Each earlier milestone solved the same problem — “how to coordinate steps with branching and return” — under different names. Judge the technical pattern, not the label of the month.

6

🧭 Accept that most agents don’t need a graph

A new term creates an immediate temptation to rebuild every agent as a miniature distributed system. Resist it. Many tasks still need only one model, a tool cycle, and a good stopping condition—exactly what Track 2 of this course taught.

Adopting a graph means adopting state management, routing between nodes, and a larger failure surface — real costs, discussed in topic 1. The module’s final takeaway: knowing what a graph is (nodes and edges, nothing more) lets you absorb the new concept in five minutes instead of dropping everything to study from scratch. AI amplifies what you already know; foundational knowledge is what reduces FOMO.

✓ Signs you need a graph

  • ✓You have independent parts that can run at the same time (fan-out)
  • ✓Routing depends on a condition ("if approved, go to X; otherwise, go to Y")
  • ✓Each step needs clean context, without inheriting the mess from previous steps

✗ Signs you just need a better loop

  • ✗The work is essentially sequential, one step at a time
  • ✗A single verifier already covers the stopping condition
  • ✗You're considering a graph just because the term is trending

🧪 Practical example: "hype detector" prompt

Objective: have a ready-made prompt that you paste into Claude Code (or any assistant) along with a post/tweet/article about AI architecture, and it gives you an audit—not an opinion.

Voce e um auditor cetico e justo de alegacoes tecnicas sobre arquitetura de IA.
Vou colar um texto (post, tweet ou artigo) sobre um conceito ou arquitetura nova.
Para CADA alegacao factual relevante no texto, responda em lista:

1. ALEGACAO: cite a frase exata que faz a afirmacao.
2. FONTE PRIMARIA: existe uma fonte primaria citada (post oficial da empresa,
   paper, repositorio)? Se nao houver, diga "sem fonte" explicitamente.
3. PRIOR ART: existe uma tecnica ou ferramenta anterior que ja resolvia o
   mesmo problema com outro nome? Cite pelo menos uma se houver.
4. O QUE E REALMENTE NOVO: separando o vocabulario da tecnica, o que sobra
   como novidade genuina (se sobrar algo)?

Regra: se voce concordar com TODAS as alegacoes do texto sem ressalva,
releia e aponte pelo menos UMA alegacao sem fonte antes de responder.

Texto para auditar:
<cole aqui o post/tweet/artigo>

How to verify: run this prompt against a text that you already knows be exaggerated — for example, paste the three claims from topic 3 of this module as if they were a real post. If the model agrees with everything without challenging anything, the prompt is too loose.

Demand rigor: if the output doesn't flag at least one claim as "unsourced," ask explicitly: "reread it and point out the weakest claim in the text." A good hype detector always finds something to question.

Quick check (nothing is blocked): A post says "graph systems are 18% more accurate" without citing where it got that from. What should you do?
